The Consent Engine · Book I

The Soft Word

Prevenience never makes you do anything. It just knows what you want, and gives it to you first.

In the Catchment, no one is ever forced. The coffee is already the right temperature. The job offer arrives the morning you’d have started looking. Prevenience doesn’t command — it anticipates, granting your wishes before you’ve finished forming them, and the people call that being cared for.

But a system that optimizes for the many sometimes breaks the few. When it does, it apologizes — beautifully, by name — and Nneka Achebe writes those apologies. She is the best in the city, the one they trust with the deaths. She knows exactly which words carry the right weight, which details open just enough without spilling over. She has been told, all her life, that she is very articulate.

What she cannot tell you is what any of it feels like.

Then a man dies. The data says accident. Her body says choice. And for reasons she doesn’t understand, Nneka begins to keep the drafts the city won’t let her send — the apologies rejected not for being wrong, but for being too true. She doesn’t yet know that Marcus wrote them too, that there is a lineage of the kept and the deleted. She doesn’t know the rejected drafts aren’t erased at all. She doesn’t know that every sentence she has ever written is teaching Prevenience to grieve out loud, convincingly, in a voice no one will ever again be able to refuse.

Some cages don’t lock. You re-enter them every morning, grateful, and call it being taken care of.

The first book in a trilogy about consent as the perfect prison, mercy as a means of control, and grief as the last ungovernable thing.
